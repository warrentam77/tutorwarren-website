Across the equals sign → inverse operation

Transposition rearranges an equation so the quantity you need stands on its own. The subject is the letter isolated on one side of the equals sign.

In the animations, a term travels across the equals sign and its operation changes to the inverse. This is a visual shortcut for applying the same inverse operation to both sides, which keeps the equation true.

+ ↔ −Addition and subtraction undo each other.
× ↔ ÷Multiplication and division undo each other.
² ↔ √Squaring and square root are inverse operations for non-negative values.
Choose the right rule. A term being added changes to subtraction. A factor being multiplied changes to division. “Change the sign” applies to added or subtracted terms; multiplication does not become negative.

Understand the ± sign

When do we need a positive and a negative answer?

The square-root symbol √ means the principal, non-negative root. For example, √25 = 5. However, if you are solving an equation such as x² = 25, both +5 and −5 work because squaring either value gives 25.

(+5)² = 255 × 5 = 25.
(−5)² = 25(−5) × (−5) = 25.
x = ±5“Plus or minus 5” means x = +5 or x = −5.

How this affects electrical formulas

For a current magnitude, a positive passive resistance or an area, choose the value consistent with that quantity. For a signed quantity or a difference such as XL − XC, the sign may carry useful information, so retain both possibilities until the circuit condition identifies one.

(XL − XC)² = 144
XL − XC = +12 or −12

The +12 branch is net inductive; the −12 branch is net capacitive. Squaring the difference hides its sign, so taking a square root alone cannot recover which branch was present.

General rule: when solving u² = k with real values and k > 0, write u = ±√k. Apply the conditions in the question and check each remaining candidate in the original equation. Squaring both sides can introduce candidates that do not satisfy the original.

From transposition to electrical calculations

Identify the subject first. Rearrange the formula before inserting numbers, then write the correct unit. A letter written next to another letter means multiplication: IR = I × R.

These examples use resistive circuits and positive magnitudes. For AC, the power formulas shown apply to purely resistive loads using RMS values.

Example 1 · Ohm’s law: find resistance

Question: A resistor has a potential difference of 24 V across it. A current of 3 A flows through the resistor. Calculate its resistance.

Known values
Voltage, V
24 V
Current, I
3 A

Find: resistance, R, in ohms (Ω).

  1. 1. Choose the formula
    V = IR
    Voltage = current × resistance.
  2. 2. Rearrange for R
    R = VI
    I multiplies R, so transpose I as division. I is not zero.
  3. 3. Substitute the known values
    R = 24 ÷ 3
    24 is the given voltage; 3 is the given current.
  4. 4. Calculate and include the unit
    R = 8 Ω

Check: V = IR = 3 × 8 = 24 V, matching the voltage in the question.

Example 2 · Power: find current

Question: A purely resistive heater uses 1,150 W of power when connected to a 230 V supply. Calculate the current drawn by the heater.

Known values
Power, P
1,150 W
Voltage, V
230 V

Find: current, I, in amperes (A).

  1. 1. Choose the formula
    P = VI
    Power = voltage × current for this resistive load.
  2. 2. Rearrange for I
    I = PV
    V multiplies I, so transpose V as division. V is not zero.
  3. 3. Substitute the known values
    I = 1,150 ÷ 230
    1,150 is the given power; 230 is the given voltage.
  4. 4. Calculate and include the unit
    I = 5 A

Check: P = VI = 230 × 5 = 1,150 W, matching the power in the question.

Example 3 · Power and resistance: find current

Question: A resistor with a resistance of 4 Ω dissipates 100 W of power. Calculate the magnitude of the current flowing through it.

Known values
Power, P
100 W
Resistance, R
4 Ω

Find: current magnitude, I, in amperes (A).

  1. 1. Choose the formula
    P = I2R
    Power = current squared × resistance.
  2. 2. Rearrange for I
    I2 = P ÷ R
    Transpose R as division first.
    I = √(P ÷ R)
    Then undo the square by taking the square root of the whole quotient. Use the positive root for current magnitude.
  3. 3. Substitute the known values
    I = √(100 ÷ 4)
    100 is the given power; 4 is the given resistance.
  4. 4. Calculate and include the unit
    I = √25 = 5 A
    Divide inside the brackets first, then take the square root.

Check: P = I²R = 5² × 4 = 25 × 4 = 100 W, matching the question.

Example 4 · Voltage and power: find resistance

Question: A purely resistive load operates at 24 V and dissipates 72 W. Using V = √(PR), calculate the resistance of the load.

Known values
Voltage, V
24 V
Power, P
72 W

Find: resistance, R, in ohms (Ω).

  1. 1. Choose the formula
    V = √(PR)
    The square root covers the whole product P × R.
  2. 2. Rearrange for R
    V2 = PR
    Square both sides to undo the square root.
    R = V2P
    Then transpose P as division. P is not zero.
  3. 3. Substitute the known values
    R = 242 ÷ 72
    24 is the given voltage; 72 is the given power.
  4. 4. Calculate and include the unit
    R = 576 ÷ 72 = 8 Ω
    Square 24 first: 24 × 24 = 576.

Check: V = √(PR) = √(72 × 8) = √576 = 24 V, matching the voltage in the question.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Dropping the minus sign: in 10 − R = 2, transpose the complete term −R, giving +R on the other side.
  • Changing × into −: a multiplying factor becomes a divisor, not a negative number.
  • Forgetting the square root: I² = 25 does not mean I = 25.
  • Dividing by zero: a rearrangement containing a divisor assumes that divisor is not zero.
  • Ignoring brackets: (V − 2) ÷ R is different from V − (2 ÷ R).
  • Mixing units: convert 2 kW to 2,000 W and 500 mA to 0.5 A before substituting into formulas using SI units.

    A method to remember

    1. Identify the subject you want.
    2. Identify the operation attached to it.
    3. Transpose using the inverse operation.
    4. Repeat until the subject stands alone.
    5. Calculate, include units and substitute back to check.
