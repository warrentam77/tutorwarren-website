01 · Build the understanding

The principles behind a lighting circuit

A lamp operates when a potential difference is applied across it and a complete circuit allows current to flow. In a typical UK single-phase supply, the nominal voltage between line and neutral is 230 V AC at 50 Hz. The lamp transfers electrical energy into light and heat. In an LED fitting, an electronic driver controls the current through the LEDs.

The complete load path is: supply line → protective device → switch contact(s) → lamp → neutral → supply. The switch and lamp are in series within this branch. Additional lamps are normally connected in parallel, so each receives the supply voltage rather than sharing it as a series chain would.

Permanent line

The line conductor upstream of the switch remains energised while the circuit supply is on, even when the lamp is off. It supplies the switch common terminal.

Switched line

The conductor downstream of the switching arrangement supplies the lamp line terminal when the contacts form a complete path. “SL” or “S/L” means switched line.

Neutral

Neutral completes the normal load-current path. It carries current when the lamp operates. It is a live conductor in electrical terminology and must never be assumed safe to touch.

CPC

The circuit protective conductor provides the protective connection for exposed conductive parts. It does not normally carry lamp load current. During an earth fault it forms part of the fault path that enables protective disconnection.

Why the switch interrupts line

Opening the line conductor removes the normal line supply from the lamp. Switching only neutral can leave the lamp connected to permanent line even though it is dark. An ordinary wall switch controls operation; it does not provide a safe-isolation procedure.

What an open or closed contact does

Closed: a conductive connection completes the load circuit, allowing current through the lamp. Open: a gap interrupts this path, so normal lamp current stops. Voltage may still exist across the gap and upstream conductors remain energised.

AC current and the moving dots

The animations use slow moving dots to help trace a complete circuit. Actual AC current reverses direction every half-cycle: at 50 Hz that is 100 reversals per second. Dots travelling one way are teaching indicators, not literal electron motion. Current is not “used up” by the lamp; energy is transferred, and the same branch current passes through its line and neutral connections.

Workshop use: these are learning diagrams. Work on a dead training circuit under appropriate supervision. Before practical wiring, follow safe isolation, secure against reconnection and prove dead with suitable equipment. The illustrated 6 A device in the supplied animations is an example, not a universal design specification; circuit design and protective measures must be verified separately.

02 · One switch, one lighting point

One-way lighting: the 2-plate method

One-way switching means controlling a lamp or group of lamps from one location. A simple one-way switch makes or breaks the connection between C and L1. A two-way accessory can also be used for one-way operation using C and one selected output, following the manufacturer’s markings.

In the feed-to-switch 2-plate arrangement shown here, the supply cable enters the switch position. Permanent line connects to C. L1 provides the switched line to the rose lamp-L group. Neutral is joined in a suitable insulated connector in the switch enclosure and continues to rose N; it does not connect to the ordinary mechanical switch contacts.

At the lighting point only the switched-line and neutral groups are used for the lamp circuit, hence “2-plate”. A three-plate rose may still be physically fitted, with LOOP unused. Other wiring layouts can also provide two-plate connections; this lesson follows your attached feed-to-switch animation.

Open contact = lamp OFF. Filled connection dots/circles indicate terminals; crossing lines without dots do not connect. CPC routes are spread out for clarity; earth connections continue to Class I fittings.

Trace the connections

  1. Supply brown → switch C.
  2. Switch L1 → brown in the outgoing twin & earth → rose lamp L.
  3. Supply blue → insulated neutral joint → outgoing blue → rose N.
  4. Lamp flex line → rose lamp L; lamp flex neutral → rose N.
  5. Maintain CPC continuity and connect protective terminals as required.

Switch closed: C connects to L1, completing the path through the lamp and neutral. Switch open: the line path breaks at the switch. The supply side of C remains energised.

03 · Same control, different joint location

One-way lighting: the 3-plate method

In the loop-in 3-plate arrangement the supply arrives at the ceiling rose. Three separate insulated terminal groups serve permanent line (LOOP), switched line (lamp L), and neutral (N). The protective terminal is separate and is not included in the “three plates” count.

Supply line joins LOOP. A brown conductor takes permanent line from LOOP down to switch C. When C–L1 closes, current can return up the blue core identified brown to rose lamp L. This blue core is switched line, not neutral. The neutral stays at the rose and connects directly to lamp neutral.

Open contact = lamp OFF. Filled connection dots/circles indicate terminals; crossing lines without dots do not connect. CPC routes are spread out for clarity; earth connections continue to Class I fittings.

Trace the connections

  1. Supply line → rose LOOP; supply neutral → rose N.
  2. Rose LOOP → brown switch-drop core → switch C.
  3. Switch L1 → blue switch-drop core with brown identification at both ends → rose lamp L.
  4. Rose lamp L → lamp flex line; rose N → lamp flex neutral.
  5. Maintain CPC continuity at rose, switch position and relevant exposed conductive parts.

Where the circuit continues to another lighting point, onward permanent line joins LOOP, onward neutral joins N and the CPC continues. These onward cables are omitted from the diagrams for clarity. Each lighting branch can then be switched independently.

The common mistake: putting the blue switch-return core in N because it is blue. It belongs in lamp L after identification as line. Connecting a switched return to neutral can create a short circuit when the switch closes.
Memory hook: “LOOP feeds down; switched line comes back; neutral stays at the rose.”

05 · Two locations, one lamp

Two-way switching and the concept of strappers

Two-way switching allows either of two switches to change the state of the same lamp, for example at the top and bottom of stairs. Each switch has C, L1 and L2. It is a changeover switch: C connects to L1 in one position and to L2 in the other. Unlike a simple one-way switch, neither normal settled position leaves C disconnected from both outputs.

What are strappers?

Strappers are the pair of conductors linking the selectable contacts of the two switches. In this example, strapper 1 links L1 to L1, and strapper 2 links L2 to L2. They provide two alternative paths between the switches. Switch 1 selects which strapper receives permanent line; switch 2 selects which strapper connects to the lamp’s switched line.

If both switches select the same strapper, there is a complete path through the lamp. If they select different strappers, that path is broken. Operating either switch changes the selection and therefore changes the lamp from ON to OFF or OFF to ON.

CPC omitted from this functional view only: maintain protective continuity to both switch positions, the rose and exposed conductive parts as required. Brown sleeving marks blue, black and grey cores used as line conductors.

In the attached arrangement, a twin & earth switch drop runs from the rose to Switch 1, and a 3-core & earth cable runs from Switch 1 to Switch 2. The three insulated cores between switches have three different duties:

Trace one ON path

Supply L → rose LOOP → brown drop → Switch 1 C → C–L1 contact → brown strapper → Switch 2 L1 → L1–C contact → grey return → insulated joint → brown-identified blue return → rose lamp L → lamp → rose N → supply neutral.

Why not just put two one-way switches in series?

In series, both one-way switches would have to be closed. If one were open, operating the other could not turn the lamp on. The two changeover switches and their alternative strapper paths allow either position to change the lamp state independently.

07 · Explain it back

Check your understanding

Try each question before opening the explanation.

Why can a lamp be off while the rose still contains live conductors?

The switch interrupts the switched branch. Permanent line at LOOP remains energised while the circuit supply is on. A dark lamp does not prove dead.

What does “3-plate” count?

The three separate insulated connection groups: permanent line, switched line and neutral. The protective terminal is separate.

Why is the blue switch-drop core brown sleeved in the 3-plate circuit?

It is used as switched line rather than neutral. The brown identification communicates its line function at its terminations.

What do the two strappers connect?

They link the two switches’ selectable contacts: L1 to L1 and L2 to L2 in the arrangement shown.

Is the grey third core another strapper?

No. In this arrangement it carries switched line from Switch 2 C back to the connector at Switch 1, then via the blue core identified brown to rose L.

Switch 1 selects L2 and Switch 2 selects L1. What happens?

The lamp is OFF because the switches select different paths. Operate either switch and both select the same path, turning the lamp ON.

Does the CPC carry the normal lamp return current?

No. Neutral carries normal load current. The CPC provides protective continuity and a fault-current path.

Where would an intermediate switch fit if a third control position were needed?

Between the two end two-way switches, in the strapper paths. It connects the pair straight through or crosses them. The end switches remain two-way switches.
